## Intro

I've always been enamored with big, custom-built desks, the kind that look like they were made for the room instead of just placed in it. Growing up, I'd watch old period films and linger on the home offices with sprawling built-in desks that stretched across an entire wall, telling myself that one day I'd have one of my own.

Now that I work from home, that daydream became practical. I wanted a space I could actually be proud of, not a desk shoved into the corner of a spare bedroom. So I remodeled the whole room myself: new flooring, insulated walls, ethernet run behind the drywall, and finally, the custom desk and shelving I'd wanted since I was a kid. This is that story.

This post is less a polished how-to and more a stream of consciousness, written mainly for myself. It's a chance to look back on what I'm proud of and what I'd do differently next time. The whole project was a learning process, and this is me working through it.

## Assessing the Room

### Requirements

Before any of that could happen, I had to figure out what this room actually needed, starting with the walls. One wall in particular stood out: it was shared with the laundry room, and the cavity behind it wasn't insulated at all. If this space was going to be my office, the sound of the washer and dryer running mid-call was a dealbreaker. That muffled thump had to go.

Once I looked closer, tearing that wall down made sense on every level. With the drywall off, I'd have full access to the cavity, so I could run new electrical and ethernet lines while everything was already exposed, then insulate properly with soundboard and green glue before closing it back up. Doing it all at once meant I wasn't opening the same wall twice. One project solved the noise problem, upgraded the wiring, and set the foundation for the rest of the room.

## Wall Renovation

### Tear Down

My plans kept changing throughout the tear down of this wall. Initially the plan was to just open the wall from midway down. This would allow us to access what we needed to and possibly reuse the drywall if we took it down gently. But the sounds of the washer and dryer running kept haunting me, so we decided to remove the entire wall that way we could build it back better with sound proofing in mind.

### Running the Wires

With the wall open, I took the opportunity to add a double-gang box with four extra outlets to power my equipment, since the base cabinets would end up covering the two existing ones. It was an easy addition: splice into the wire running between the existing boxes and drop in the new one.

The wall sconces were a stroke of luck. When I moved in, I noticed one of two switches by the bedroom door did nothing, while the other controlled the ceiling light. My dad and I figured out the room had been wired for a ceiling fan with a light, but only a light fixture was ever installed, leaving that second switch dead. Behind the fixture we found the capped-off wires waiting to be used. I ran fresh 12 AWG wire through the attic alongside the ethernet cable and into the wall cavity, and just like that, the dead switch had a job again.
